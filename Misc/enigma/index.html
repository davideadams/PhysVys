Enigma Machine

Info

A working Enigma I, M3 or M4. Choose the rotors, ring settings, reflector and plugboard cables from a real key sheet, turn the rotors to the start position, then type on your keyboard (or click the keys). Each key press turns the rotors first, then lights a lamp. Typing the ciphertext back in with the same start position gives the message, because the machine is its own inverse. The wiring diagram at the bottom follows the current for the last key through every part of the machine.

The ring setting (Ringstellung) turns the letter ring against the wiring inside. Picking a rotor that is already in use swaps the two.

Or click two sockets on the plugboard to join them with a cable.

Each column is one part of the machine, with its 26 contacts. The rotor columns show the letters on the rotor's ring, so they move up by one each time that rotor turns. The reflector sends the current back by a different route, which is why a letter can never light its own lamp.

Why it was hard to break

  • The alphabet changes every key press. The right rotor turns before each letter, so pressing A five times gives five different letters. With three rotors it takes 16 900 presses (26 × 25 × 26, because of the double step) before the rotors come back round.
  • Huge number of settings. For the Enigma I with ten plugboard cables: 60 rotor orders × 17 576 start positions × 676 ring settings that matter × about 151 million million plugboard wirings. That is around 1023 keys.
  • The weakness. No letter can ever encrypt to itself. If codebreakers guessed a word in the message (a crib, such as WETTERBERICHT, “weather report”), they could rule out every place it would put a letter on top of itself. Turing and Welchman's bombes at Bletchley Park used cribs like this to find the day's key.
