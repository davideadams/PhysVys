Classroom Ciphers

Info

One message, eight pen-and-paper ciphers. Type into the box, pick a cipher and a direction, and the answer updates as you type. Switching cipher keeps your message, so the same sentence can be shown several ways. Under the answer, Pen & paper shows the working a student would do by hand: the shifted alphabet, the key written under the message, the Playfair square and its pairs, the Polybius grid, the scytale's rod and strip, the Morse chart and the pigpen grids.

A shift of 13 is its own inverse: encoding twice gives the message back.

The keyword (repeats crossed out) goes under A, B, C…, then the unused letters follow in order.

Each letter of the keyword is a Caesar shift (A = 0, B = 1, …). The keyword repeats along the message, skipping spaces and punctuation.

I and J share a square, so J is written as I. A doubled filler (XX) is split with Q instead.

Drops a filler sitting between two identical letters, and one at the very end. It is a guess: a real X in those spots goes too.

26 letters do not fit in 25 squares, so two share. A 6 × 6 square has room for every letter and digit (26 + 10 = 36).

Blank gives the alphabet in order. A keyword mixes the square, the same way as Playfair.

A thicker rod fits more letters in each turn of the strip. Only letters and digits are written on the strip.

Without it, the strip just stops part-way round the rod.

A dash lasts three dots. Gaps: one dot inside a letter, three between letters, seven between words.

Pigpen has no digits or punctuation. Those are shown as typed.
